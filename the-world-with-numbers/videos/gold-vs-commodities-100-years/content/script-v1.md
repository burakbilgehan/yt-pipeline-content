## Act 2: The Gold Standard Era — 1925–1971 (0:30 – 1:15)

[VOICEOVER]
For the first half of this story, gold's price was literally fixed. Twenty dollars and sixty-seven cents per ounce. The government said so. That made the Dow-to-Gold ratio a clean reflection of the stock market's real performance.

In 1925, the ratio stood at six. Then the Great Depression hit and crushed it. In 1934, Roosevelt devalued the dollar — gold jumped to thirty-five dollars an ounce overnight — and the ratio dropped to about three.

After World War Two, the Bretton Woods system pegged the entire global economy to gold. The ratio slowly climbed back. By the late 1960s, American stocks were soaring, and the Dow-to-Gold ratio was pushing past twenty. Everything looked great — on paper.

But behind the scenes, the U.S. was printing money to fund the Vietnam War and social programs. The gold standard was breaking.

## Act 3: Nixon Shock & Gold's Liberation — 1971–1980 (1:15 – 2:00)

[VOICEOVER]
On August fifteenth, nineteen seventy-one, President Nixon went on television and severed the dollar from gold. Just like that — the gold standard was dead.

Gold was free. And it exploded. From thirty-five dollars to over eight hundred in less than a decade. The Dow-to-Gold ratio went into freefall — from twenty down to almost one. Let me say that again. On January twenty-second, nineteen eighty, one ounce of gold could buy the entire Dow Jones Industrial Average. One to one.

And it wasn't just stocks. Every single asset — oil, silver, copper, platinum — every ratio bottomed out at the same time. Gold crushed everything simultaneously.

This is the most dramatic moment in a hundred years of financial data.

By August twenty-fifth, nineteen ninety-nine, the Dow-to-Gold ratio hit its all-time peak: forty-four point eight eight. Gold's worst day in a century. One ounce of gold could only buy a tiny fraction of the Dow. Stocks were king. Nobody wanted gold.

Even Germany's DAX lost twenty-nine percent in gold terms. The only standout winner? Japan's Nikkei — up nine hundred and twenty-two percent in gold terms — the post-war economic miracle priced in the hardest money on earth.
