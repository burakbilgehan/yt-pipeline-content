## Section: The Salary Illusion (0:30-2:15)

[VISUAL NOTE] Split screen: a three-thousand-dollar stack of cash on the left. On the right, two cities — Zurich and Mexico City — with local prices appearing: rent, groceries, a Big Mac. The cash visually "shrinks" in Zurich and "grows" in Mexico City.

[VOICEOVER]
Three thousand dollars a month. <break time="0.5s" /> In parts of Mexico, that covers rent, food, transport, and savings. In Zurich, it barely covers a studio apartment. Same number. Completely different lives. <break time="0.8s" /> This is why economists use something called Purchasing Power Parity, or PPP — a way to adjust salaries so they reflect what money actually buys in each country. The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, or OECD, publishes PPP-adjusted average wages for over thirty member countries every year. <break time="0.5s" /> After PPP adjustment, an American earns eighty-two thousand nine hundred thirty-three dollars. A German earns sixty-nine thousand four hundred thirty-three. A Mexican earns twenty thousand four hundred thirty-three. <break time="0.5s" /> These numbers are more honest — they account for local prices. But they're still abstract. [thoughtful] Forty-nine thousand dollars PPP in Japan versus sixty-nine thousand in Germany — what does that difference actually feel like when you're standing in a grocery store? <break time="0.5s" /> We need something more tangible.

## Section: The Big Mac Lens (2:15-4:30)

[VISUAL NOTE] A Big Mac appears center screen. Price tags pop up around it for different countries: USA five seventy-nine, Japan three eleven, Switzerland seven ninety-nine, Euro area five ninety-five. The prices animate into a horizontal bar chart.

[VOICEOVER]
[curious] This is where the Big Mac comes in. <break time="0.5s" /> Since nineteen eighty-six, The Economist has tracked the price of a Big Mac in over fifty countries. A Big Mac uses local labor, local ingredients, local rent — making it a useful proxy for local cost of living. <break time="0.8s" /> In the United States, a Big Mac costs five dollars and seventy-nine cents. In Japan, three dollars and eleven cents. In Switzerland, seven dollars and ninety-nine cents. <break time="0.5s" /> Take a country's PPP-adjusted hourly wage and divide it by the local Big Mac price. What you get is a single number: how many Big Macs one hour of work can buy. <break time="1.0s" /> This captures both how much you earn AND how far that money goes. <break time="0.5s" /> And this is where the rankings start to shift.

[VISUAL NOTE] Animated ranking: countries re-sort from raw PPP wage ranking to Big Macs per hour ranking. Switzerland drops from second to fifteenth. Japan jumps from mid-pack to second. Luxembourg stays at the top.

[VOICEOVER]
Switzerland has the second-highest PPP-adjusted wage in the OECD — eighty-seven thousand four hundred sixty-eight dollars, behind only Luxembourg. <break time="0.5s" /> But a Big Mac there costs seven ninety-nine. Divide the hourly wage by that price and Switzerland drops to number fifteen — seven point one six Big Macs per hour. <break time="0.8s" /> Japan earns about fifty-seven percent of Switzerland's wage — forty-nine thousand four hundred forty-six dollars. But a Big Mac costs just three eleven. [thoughtful] The result? Number two in the world at nine point eight nine Big Macs per hour. <break time="1.0s" /> High salary does not mean high purchasing power. And low salary does not always mean low purchasing power. The price of what you buy matters just as much as what you earn.
