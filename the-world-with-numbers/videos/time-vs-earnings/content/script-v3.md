[VOICEOVER]
Three thousand dollars a month. [pause] In parts of Mexico, that covers rent, food, transport, and leaves room for savings. In Zurich, it barely covers a studio apartment. [pause] Same number. Completely different lives. [pause long] This is the problem with comparing salaries across countries. A dollar in Tokyo does not buy what a dollar buys in Warsaw — or in New York. [pause] So economists use something called Purchasing Power Parity — or PPP. [pause short] Here's how it works. [pause] The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development tracks the prices of roughly three thousand goods and services in each member country — everything from bread and rice to haircuts, electricity, and rent. They use those prices to calculate a conversion factor for every country. [pause long] When the OECD says a Swiss worker earns eighty-seven thousand four hundred sixty-eight PPP dollars, it means that salary buys the same amount of stuff as eighty-seven thousand four hundred sixty-eight dollars would buy in the United States. [pause] A German earning sixty-nine thousand four hundred thirty-three PPP dollars has the same purchasing power as an American earning sixty-nine thousand four hundred thirty-three dollars in America. [pause long] Apples to apples. [pause long] After this adjustment, the rankings shift. Luxembourg leads at ninety-four thousand four hundred forty-seven.
