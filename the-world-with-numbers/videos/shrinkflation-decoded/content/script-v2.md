### HOOK (0:00–0:30)

[VISUAL: All 6 product icons at starting line, RUPI = 1.0. Title fades in: "Shrinkflation Decoded." Baseline reference line pulses.]

[VOICEOVER]
<speak>
<p>
Your bag of chips is smaller. Your coffee can lost a third of its weight. Everyone says groceries are more expensive. <break time="500ms"/> But when you measure the actual price per unit against median wages... four out of six common grocery products cost LESS of your paycheck today than they did in the year two thousand. <break time="1000ms"/> The data says you're winning. <break time="500ms"/> So why doesn't it feel that way?
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE METRIC (0:30–1:40)

[VISUAL: Animated formula build — nominal price ÷ wage growth = RUPI. Baseline reference at 1.0 with green zone below, red zone above. BLS logo and data source footnote. When shrinkflation is mentioned, brief animation: a coffee can visually shrinks while the price tag stays the same, then a "per pound" price counter ticks upward.]

[VOICEOVER]
<speak>
<p>
Imagine you earned five hundred seventy-two dollars a week in two thousand. A dozen eggs cost ninety-one cents. Today you earn twelve hundred — but eggs cost four twenty-five. Did eggs get more expensive... or did your paycheck grow faster? <break time="500ms"/> That's the question behind what we call the Real Unit Price Index — RUPI. Take twenty-five years of Bureau of Labor Statistics prices — per pound, per gallon, per dozen — and divide each product's price growth by median wage growth.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
One means a product costs the same share of your paycheck as in two thousand. Above one — it's taking a bigger bite. Below one — wages won.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Now — a common objection. If companies are shrinking packages, doesn't that hide inflation? Actually, the BLS already accounts for this. When your coffee can shrinks from sixteen ounces to ten at the same sticker price, BLS records that as a price INCREASE per pound. Shrinkflation is already baked into the numbers you're about to see.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Six products. All starting at one point zero. <break time="1000ms"/> Let the race begin.
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]

[NOTE: The term RUPI is introduced here and used throughout. The shrinkflation-in-BLS explanation addresses the viewer's most likely objection before the race starts.]

### THE RACE — ACT 1: 2000–2009 (1:40–3:40)

[VISUAL: Race chart animates from 2000. All lines start at 1.0. Chapter marker: "ACT 1: THE DIVERGENCE." Eggs spike upward in 2003–2004 and again 2007–2008. Coffee drifts down. Other products hover near baseline. Brief "how to read" overlay at 2001: arrow pointing up labeled "More expensive relative to wages," arrow pointing down labeled "Cheaper relative to wages," horizontal line labeled "Same as 2000." Shrinkflation overlay annotations appear: "2009: Häagen-Dazs 16→14 oz" and "2009: Skippy 18→16.3 oz".]

[VOICEOVER]
<speak>
<p>
Two thousand to two thousand nine. <break time="500ms"/> Right away, eggs break from the pack. An avian flu scare in two thousand three pushes them up. By two thousand four, they're already about thirty percent above the baseline — more of your paycheck for the same dozen. Then a global commodity surge in two thousand seven and eight drives them higher. By two thousand eight, eggs are the clear outlier — nearly seventy-five percent more of your weekly wage than they cost in two thousand.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Coffee does the opposite. It drifts steadily downward through the early two thousands. By two thousand four, a pound of ground roast takes about a quarter less of your paycheck than it did at the start of the decade. Coffee kept getting cheaper.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Chips, milk, peanut butter, ice cream — they all hover near the baseline. Wages are roughly keeping pace. Nothing dramatic. Nothing alarming.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Then two thousand eight — the financial crisis. Milk and chips push briefly above one as commodity prices surge, but by two thousand nine, most products settle back down.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
And behind the scenes — something quieter is happening. Häagen-Dazs shrinks its container from sixteen to fourteen ounces. Skippy redesigns its peanut butter jar — same height, deeper indent in the bottom — dropping from eighteen to sixteen point three ounces. <break time="500ms"/> Why? Because three ninety-nine is a magic number. Consumers notice when a price crosses four dollars. They don't notice when two ounces disappear. The BLS tracks price per unit, so the shrinkage shows up in the data. But the sticker price at the register? That barely moved.
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE RACE — ACT 2: 2010–2019 (3:40–5:50)

[VISUAL: Race chart continues. Chapter marker: "ACT 2: THE QUIET DECADE." Coffee spikes above 1.0 in 2011–2012, then falls back. Eggs volatile but elevated, peaking visibly in 2015. Everything else slowly drifts downward. By 2019, most products cluster well below the baseline. Shrinkflation overlay: "Doritos: 12oz → 9.25oz" and "Folgers: 16oz → 10.3oz". Subtle annotation at 2019: "4 of 6 below 1.0."]

[VOICEOVER]
<speak>
<p>
The twenty-tens. The quiet decade. <break time="500ms"/> Coffee spikes above one in twenty eleven and twelve — a global commodity boom — then falls back below. It would stay below one for years. Eggs remain volatile. The twenty fifteen avian flu outbreak pushes them nearly twice as expensive as the baseline — then they crash back down by twenty seventeen.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
But look at the rest of the field. They're drifting down. Slowly. Steadily. No single dramatic moment — just a gradual slide. Peanut butter. Milk. Chips. Ice cream. All sinking below one. Wages growing just a little faster than these prices, year after year.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Why? The post-recovery economy. Unemployment fell. Labor markets tightened, especially after twenty sixteen. Wages started climbing — not dramatically, but persistently. And food commodity prices stayed relatively flat.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
By twenty nineteen, every product sits below the baseline — most of them well below it. The chart says things are getting better. It looks like the story has a happy ending. <break time="1000ms"/>
</p>
<p>
Except — look at the shelf, not the chart. On the chart, chips sit just below the line — barely cheaper per ounce. But on the shelf, a standard Doritos bag reportedly shrank from twelve ounces to nine and a quarter. Twenty-three percent less product. Folgers, according to consumer tracking, went from a full one-pound can to ten point three ounces — thirty-six percent less coffee in the same-looking can.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
According to the BLS, the snack food category alone saw a cumulative two point six four percent price impact from downsizing between twenty fifteen and twenty nineteen. The price per ounce held steady. The package just got smaller. <break time="500ms"/>
</p>
<p>
And there's one more thing the data can't capture. Skimpflation. Same package, same size, same price — but cheaper ingredients. Thinner chips. Waterier ice cream. Less cocoa in the chocolate. Unlike shrinkflation, there's no government data series for this. No BLS measurement. It's real — economists acknowledge it — but it lives in the gap between what statistics can track and what your taste buds notice. <break time="500ms"/>

If you were shopping during this decade, it probably didn't FEEL like things were getting cheaper. And in one sense, they weren't — per trip to the store. You were buying the same number of packages, paying roughly the same at checkout, getting less in the bag each time, and sometimes getting a lesser product too. The per-unit math works out. The grocery receipt doesn't care about per-unit math. <break time="1000ms"/>
</p>
<p>
The lines are drifting down. Everything looks calm.
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE RACE — ACT 3: 2020–2025 (5:50–7:50)

[VISUAL: Chapter marker: "ACT 3: THE RECKONING." 2020 dip with COVID annotation: "2020: COVID wage anomaly — median jumped as lower-wage workers lost jobs." 2022: inflation surge — all lines rise. Eggs explode upward. 2024-2025: eggs keep climbing. Coffee crosses above 1.0. Chart freezes at 2025 final positions. Clear visual: 2 above the line, 4 below.]

[VOICEOVER]
<speak>
<p>
Twenty twenty. <break time="500ms"/> A dip across the board. Every product appears to get cheaper overnight. But this isn't because food prices dropped. During COVID, lower-wage workers disproportionately lost their jobs — retail, hospitality, food service. The people who remained employed skewed higher-income. So the median wage jumped. Not because anyone got a raise. Because the composition of who was still working changed. <break time="500ms"/> The prices on the chart were real. The wage improvement was a statistical artifact.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
By twenty twenty-one, workers return — and the median normalizes. Then twenty twenty-two. Inflation surges. Supply chains still broken. Energy costs cascading through every product. Eggs spike — avian flu again, this time the worst outbreak in American history. Tens of millions of birds culled. <break time="500ms"/> Everything on the chart rises. For the first time in years, the lines are moving UP.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Twenty twenty-three and twenty-four — most products stabilize or edge back down. But not eggs. Avian flu keeps returning. And by twenty twenty-five, eggs have more than doubled relative to the baseline. More than doubled. The chart shows you exactly how far. This isn't about shrinkflation or corporate strategy — it's biology. Disease. The egg market runs on chickens, and the chickens keep getting sick.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Coffee crosses back above one in twenty twenty-five — the first time in over a decade. Drought in Brazil. Crop failures in Vietnam. A pound of coffee now takes a bigger slice of your paycheck than it did twenty-five years ago.
</p>
<break time="1000ms"/>
<p>
But when the chart freezes at twenty twenty-five... look at the full picture. Peanut butter — down more than a third from the baseline. The biggest winner for your wallet. Milk and ice cream — both well below one. Even chips, despite all the shrinkage, sit just below the line. <break time="500ms"/> Four out of six products. Cheaper relative to wages. After twenty-five years. After everything.
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE LENS SWITCH (7:50–11:20)

[VISUAL: Beat 1 — Chart pauses. Text: "But cheaper… for whom?" Beat 2 — Lines shift upward as deflator switches to CPI. Chips crosses 1.0. Now 3 above the line. Beat 3 — Deflator switches to minimum wage. Lines JUMP. 5 of 6 above 1.0. Eggs soar. Counter appears: "$7.25/hr — unchanged since July 2009." A running ticker: "16 years, 8 months and counting." Beat 4 — Gold deflator. All lines collapse toward zero. Brief.]

[VOICEOVER]
<speak>
<p>
Four out of six products, cheaper relative to wages. <break time="1000ms"/> But cheaper... for whom? <break time="1000ms"/> That index used median wages — the middle of the income distribution. The typical worker. What if we change the ruler?
</p>
<break time="1200ms"/>
<p>
Switch to the Consumer Price Index — CPI. This is the government's standard measure of inflation. It tracks roughly eighty thousand prices across the economy every month. When we use CPI instead of wages, we're asking a simpler question: did food prices outpace general inflation?
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
The lines shift up. Chips crosses above one. Coffee jumps higher. Eggs climb further. Now three products cost more in inflation-adjusted terms than they did twenty-five years ago. <break time="500ms"/> Why the difference? Because median wages grew faster than inflation over this period — about a hundred and eleven percent versus eighty-seven percent. The median worker gained real purchasing power. That's a good thing. But it means the wage-deflated view is the OPTIMISTIC one.
</p>
<break time="1200ms"/>
<p>
Now switch to the federal minimum wage. <break time="1000ms"/>
</p>
<p>
Seven dollars and twenty-five cents an hour. That's the federal minimum wage in the United States. It was set in July of two thousand nine. <break time="500ms"/> It hasn't changed since. Not once. Sixteen years. Through a pandemic. Through the worst inflation in forty years. Through everything. <break time="500ms"/> Roughly one point one million American workers earn exactly this amount. <break time="500ms"/> Millions more earn close to it.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Watch the lines. <break time="1000ms"/>
</p>
<p>
Five of six products jump above one. The chart transforms. What looked like a story about affordability becomes a very different story. <break time="500ms"/> Eggs — a minimum wage worker now needs more than three times the work hours to buy a dozen compared to two thousand. Think about that concretely. In two thousand, a dozen eggs at ninety-one cents cost a minimum wage worker about eleven minutes of work. Today, at four twenty-five, it costs over thirty-five minutes. Same dozen eggs. Same job. Three times the effort.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Coffee. Chips. Milk. Ice cream. All above the line. Products that looked like bargains under median wages are now MORE expensive for anyone stuck at the federal floor.
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Only peanut butter stays below — barely. Just under one. The last holdout.
</p>
<break time="1200ms"/>
<p>
The median worker gained purchasing power over twenty-five years. The minimum wage worker lost it. Same economy. Same products. Same quarter century. A completely different experience depending on where you sit in the income distribution.
</p>
<break time="1200ms"/>
<p>
And one more lens. Gold. <break time="500ms"/> If you'd measured your groceries against gold's twelve-fold rise over this period... everything collapses toward zero. Eggs, coffee, chips — all on sale. Deep, deep on sale. <break time="500ms"/> But no one buys eggs with gold.
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]

[NOTE: The minimum wage section is the emotional core of the video. The concrete "eleven minutes vs thirty-five minutes" example makes the abstraction tangible. Let the chart transformation breathe — don't rush the visual. The gold lens should feel like an afterthought — brief, philosophical, 10-15 seconds max.]

<speak>
<p>
Same six products. Same twenty-five years. Same Bureau of Labor Statistics data. <break time="500ms"/> Under median wages — four out of six got cheaper. Under CPI — three got more expensive. Under the minimum wage — five out of six. Under gold — everything's a bargain. <break time="1000ms"/>
</p>
<p>
Shrinkflation is real. Your chip bag lost twenty-three percent of its weight. Your coffee can lost more than a third. But the biggest shrinkage isn't in the package. <break time="1000ms"/>
</p>
<p>
It's in the question we forgot to ask. <break time="500ms"/> Cheaper for whom?
</p>
<break time="500ms"/>
<p>
Same data. Same math. Different ruler. <break time="500ms"/> Different reality.
</p>
</speak>
[/VOICEOVER]
