### HOOK (0:00–0:25)

[VISUAL: All 6 product icons at starting line, RUPI = 1.0. Title fades in: "Shrinkflation Decoded." Baseline reference line pulses.]

[VOICEOVER]
Your bag of chips is smaller. Your coffee can lost a third of its weight. Everyone says groceries are more expensive. [pause] But when you measure the actual price per unit against median wages... four out of six common grocery products cost LESS of your paycheck today than they did in the year two thousand. [pause long] The data says you're winning. So why doesn't it feel that way?
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE METRIC (0:25–1:15)

[VISUAL: Animated formula build — nominal price ÷ wage growth = RUPI. Baseline reference at 1.0 with green zone below, red zone above. BLS logo and data source footnote.]

[VOICEOVER]
Imagine you earned five hundred seventy-two dollars a week in two thousand. A dozen eggs cost ninety-one cents. Today you earn twelve hundred — but eggs cost four twenty-five. Did eggs get more expensive... or did your paycheck grow faster? [pause] That's the question behind what we call the Real Unit Price Index — RUPI. Take twenty-five years of Bureau of Labor Statistics prices — per pound, per gallon, per dozen — and divide each product's price growth by median wage growth. [pause] One means a product costs the same share of your paycheck as in two thousand. Above one — it's taking a bigger bite. Below one — wages won. [pause] Six products. All starting at one point zero. [pause long] Let the race begin.
[/VOICEOVER]

[NOTE: The term RUPI is introduced here and used throughout. Keep the explanation tight — viewers need just enough to read the chart.]

### THE RACE — ACT 1: 2000–2009 (1:15–2:45)

[VISUAL: Race chart animates from 2000. All lines start at 1.0. Eggs spike upward in 2003–2004 and again 2007–2008. Coffee drifts down toward 0.74. Other products hover near baseline. Shrinkflation overlay annotations appear: "2009: Häagen-Dazs 16→14 oz" and "2009: Skippy 18→16.3 oz".]

[VOICEOVER]
Two thousand to two thousand nine. [pause] Right away, eggs break from the pack. An avian flu scare in two thousand three pushes them up. Then a global commodity surge in two thousand seven and eight drives them higher — up to one point seven three by two thousand eight. Seventy-three percent more of your paycheck for a dozen eggs in just eight years. [pause] Coffee does the opposite. It drifts below zero point seven four by two thousand four. Coffee was getting cheaper year after year — a pound of ground roast taking less and less of your paycheck. [pause] Chips, milk, peanut butter, ice cream — they all hover near the baseline. Wages are roughly keeping pace. [pause] Then two thousand eight. The financial crisis. Milk and chips push briefly above one as commodity prices surge. But by two thousand nine, most products settle back down. [pause] Behind the scenes, something else is happening. Häagen-Dazs quietly shrinks its container from sixteen to fourteen ounces. Skippy redesigns its jar — same height, deeper indent — drops from eighteen to sixteen point three ounces. [pause] The BLS tracks price per unit, so the shrinkage is captured in the data. But the sticker price at the register? That barely moved. <break time="1200ms"/>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE RACE — ACT 2: 2010–2019 (2:45–4:15)

[VISUAL: Race chart continues. Coffee spikes to 1.22 in 2011–2012, then falls back. Eggs volatile but elevated, hitting 1.93 in 2015. Everything else slowly drifts downward. By 2019, most products cluster in the 0.65–0.87 range. Shrinkflation overlay: "Doritos: 12oz → 9.25oz" and "Folgers: 16oz → 10.3oz". 2020 COVID wage anomaly flagged with annotation.]

[VOICEOVER]
The twenty-tens. Coffee spikes to one point two two in twenty twelve — a global commodity boom — then falls back below one. Eggs stay volatile, peaking at one point nine three during the twenty fifteen avian flu outbreak, then crashing back down by twenty seventeen. [pause] But look at the rest of the field. They're drifting down. Peanut butter, milk, chips, ice cream — all sliding comfortably below one. By twenty nineteen, four of six products sit well under the baseline. Wages are quietly winning. [pause] It looks like the story has a happy ending. [pause long] Except — look at the shelf, not the chart. On the chart, chips sit at zero point eight four. But on the shelf, a standard Doritos bag reportedly shrank from twelve ounces to nine and a quarter. Folgers, according to consumer tracking, went from a full pound to ten point three ounces — thirty-six percent less coffee in the same-looking can. [pause] According to the BLS, the snack food category saw a cumulative two point six four percent price impact from downsizing between twenty fifteen and twenty nineteen. The price per ounce held steady. The package just got smaller. [pause long] The lines are drifting down. Everything looks calm. <break time="1200ms"/>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE RACE — ACT 3: 2020–2025 (4:15–5:30)

[VISUAL: 2020 dip with COVID annotation: "2020: COVID wage anomaly — median jumped as lower-wage workers lost jobs." 2022: inflation surge — all lines rise. Eggs explode. 2025: eggs at 2.21, coffee crosses 1.0. Chart freezes at 2025 final positions. Clear visual: 2 above the line, 4 below.]

[VOICEOVER]
Twenty twenty. A dip across the board — but not because food got cheaper. Lower-wage workers disproportionately lost jobs during COVID, pushing the remaining workers' median up. The prices were real. The wages were a statistical artifact. [pause] Then twenty twenty-two. Inflation surges. Eggs explode to one point seven one. Everything rises. [pause] By twenty twenty-five, eggs reach two point two one — more than double the share of your paycheck compared to two thousand. Driven almost entirely by severe avian flu outbreaks. Coffee crosses back above one for the first time since roughly twenty fourteen, landing at one point one three, pushed by drought in Brazil and crop failures in Vietnam. [pause long] But when the chart freezes at twenty twenty-five... four products are still below the line. Peanut butter sits lowest at zero point six four. Chips cling closest to the line at zero point nine five. Milk, ice cream — all well below one. [pause] Four out of six. Cheaper. <break time="1200ms"/>
[/VOICEOVER]

### THE LENS SWITCH (5:30–7:15)

[VISUAL: Beat 1 — Chart pauses. Text: "But cheaper… for whom?" Beat 2 — Lines shift upward as deflator switches to CPI. Chips crosses 1.0. Now 3 above the line. Beat 3 — Deflator switches to minimum wage. Lines JUMP. 5 of 6 above 1.0. Eggs at 3.31. Counter appears: "$7.25/hr — unchanged since July 2009." Beat 4 — Gold deflator. All lines collapse toward zero. Brief.]

[VOICEOVER]
Four out of six products, cheaper relative to wages. [pause long] But cheaper... for whom? [pause long] That index used median wages — the middle of the income distribution. What if we change the ruler? [pause] Switch to the Consumer Price Index. Strip out income entirely — just adjust for general inflation. The lines shift up. Chips crosses above one. Now three products cost more in real terms than they did twenty-five years ago. Eggs jump to two point four nine. [pause] Wages grew faster than inflation over this period. The median worker gained purchasing power. But not everyone did. <break time="1200ms"/> Now switch to the federal minimum wage. [pause long] Seven dollars and twenty-five cents an hour. Unchanged since July two thousand nine. Sixteen years frozen. [pause] Watch the lines. [pause long] Five of six products jump above one. Eggs hit three point three one — a minimum wage worker needs more than three times the work hours to buy a dozen eggs compared to two thousand. Coffee reaches one point six eight. Chips, milk, ice cream — all above the line now. [pause] Only peanut butter stays below, barely, at zero point nine five. <break time="1200ms"/> And one more lens. Gold. [pause] If you'd measured your groceries against gold's twelve-fold rise... everything collapses toward zero. Eggs, coffee, chips — all on sale. [pause] But no one buys eggs with gold. <break time="1200ms"/>
[/VOICEOVER]

[NOTE: The minimum wage beat is the emotional core of the video. Let the chart transformation breathe — don't rush the visual. The gold lens should feel almost like an afterthought — brief, philosophical, 10-15 seconds max.]

[/VOICEOVER]
