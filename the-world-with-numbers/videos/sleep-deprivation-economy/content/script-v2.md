## ACT 1 — THE HUMAN COST (0:45–2:45)

Let's start with what sleep deprivation actually does to people — because the economic numbers only make sense once you understand what is happening inside the body of every under-slept worker.

And we are not talking about a small group.

Seven out of ten workers say they feel tired when the workday ends. Nearly half report being regularly tired during the workday itself. This is not a minority experience. This is the baseline.

More than one in three American adults sleeps less than the recommended seven hours per night. That is not a fringe group. That is the norm.

RAND's economic model uses an even stricter threshold — under six hours per night — to define the danger zone. We will come back to why that distinction matters.

Your prefrontal cortex — the part of the brain governing decisions, impulse control, and risk assessment — shows measurably reduced activity after a bad night. Your reaction time slows. You make worse choices and you do not even know it.

After 24 hours without sleep: anxiety, disorientation, perceptual distortions. After 72 hours — and this is not science fiction, this is night-shift workers and hospital interns — symptoms resembling psychosis. Hallucinations. Delusions.

But extreme cases are not required for real damage.

Chronic short sleep — under six hours per night — raises your all-cause mortality risk by ten percent compared to people sleeping the recommended seven to nine hours. Sleep six to seven hours? Still a four percent elevated risk.

Sleep deprivation raises ghrelin, your hunger hormone, and suppresses leptin, your fullness hormone — so you eat more, gain weight, develop insulin resistance. It raises blood pressure. It impairs immune function.

And on the road: more than six thousand fatal car crashes per year in the United States are caused by drowsy driving. Not alcohol. Not drugs. Tired people.

The body keeps score. And eventually, so does the economy.

## ACT 2 — THE ECONOMIC MACHINE (2:45–5:15)

In 2016, a team at RAND Europe asked a question no one had properly quantified before: how much does sleep deprivation actually cost an economy?

They studied five countries — the United States, Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom, and Canada. They measured both absenteeism, people not showing up, and presenteeism, the quieter and more expensive problem: people showing up but performing far below their capacity.

The numbers were staggering.

The United States loses between 1.56 and 2.28 percent of GDP every year to insufficient sleep. At the high end, that is four hundred and eleven billion dollars. More than the entire annual economic output of Portugal.

Japan: between eighty-eight and one hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars. Germany: sixty billion. The UK: fifty billion. Canada: twenty-one billion.

[VISUAL: Japan bar shows $88B–$138B range, not just $138B ceiling]

Five countries. Six hundred and eighty billion dollars. Every year.

That is more than the combined GDP of Portugal and New Zealand — going up in smoke every year. Not from a war. Not from a financial crisis. From people not sleeping.

[VISUAL NOTE: Horizontal bar chart — countries ranked by absolute loss. US bar extends dramatically further. Dollar amounts appear with a ticker animation. Source: RAND RR-1791, 2016. Then cut to the Portugal + New Zealand comparison card.]

But rank those same countries by percentage of GDP lost, and something unexpected happens. Japan tops the list, at between 1.86 and 2.92 percent. Higher than the US. Higher than the UK. Higher than everyone.

Japan. The country famous for working harder than almost anyone else on earth.

There is even a Japanese word for sleeping on the job — inemuri, 居眠り, "sleeping while present." It is paradoxically tolerated as a sign of dedication — proof that you worked yourself to exhaustion. And yet RAND's data shows this culture of relentless, exhausted work is not just failing workers. It is failing the economy.

The US loses the equivalent of 1.23 million working days per year. Japan: 604,000. Germany and the UK each lose over 200,000.

And in a 2023 follow-up, RAND narrowed in further. They looked specifically at people with chronic insomnia — clinical sleep disorders, not just short sleep. Each of those individuals loses between 45 and 54 working days of productivity per year. Not collectively. Each one.

RAND's model does not even include healthcare costs from sleep-related disease — no cardiovascular bills, no diabetes treatment, no accident liability. Just lost labour. The real cost is larger.

## ACT 3 — BEYOND THE FIVE COUNTRIES (5:15–7:45)

RAND's 2016 study covered five countries. There are nearly two hundred in the world. So what does the picture look like beyond those five?

RAND's methodology is transparent enough to approximate. The structure: take a country's GDP, multiply by the percentage of its workforce sleeping under six hours per night, apply a productivity loss coefficient derived from the five-country data. That gives you an estimated economic loss.

[VISUAL NOTE: On-screen formula: GDP × (% workforce sleeping <6h) × productivity loss coefficient = estimated annual loss. Three clean boxes with arrows. Label: "RAND's framework, simplified."]

The regional data reveals a clear pattern. East Asia is at the bottom of global sleep rankings. Japan averages around six hours and twenty-two minutes per night. South Korea is comparable, around six hours and thirty minutes. These are among the shortest average sleep durations in any large national dataset.

Northern Europe sits at the other extreme. The Netherlands averages over eight hours — the highest in any major study. France and Germany cluster around seven hours.

[VISUAL NOTE: World map with gradient — East Asia in red/orange, Western Europe in cool blue/green. Sleep duration labels. Source note on screen: "Walch et al. 2016, Science Advances, as reported in secondary sources. ⚠️ UNVERIFIED direct access."]

Now take South Korea. GDP of 1.67 trillion dollars. One of the most intense work cultures in the world. OECD data shows South Korean workers average around 1,872 hours of work per year. Japan, already extreme, averages 1,626. South Korea works more.

Applying Japan's RAND-measured GDP loss percentage to South Korea's economy gives an estimated loss of 31 to 49 billion dollars per year.

[VISUAL NOTE: South Korea + Japan side-by-side comparison. Estimated loss range with clear on-screen label: "⚠️ ESTIMATED — Not a RAND-measured figure. Modelled using Japan's GDP loss ratio applied to South Korean GDP." Work hours: Korea 1,872 hrs vs Japan 1,626 hrs — OECD.]

That estimate is almost certainly conservative. South Korea works 246 more hours per year than Japan. RAND's own model identifies long work hours and high psychosocial stress as primary drivers of sleep deprivation costs. The true figure for South Korea is likely higher than what this proxy suggests.

What about India?

India's average sleep data — mostly from wearable devices skewed toward affluent, urban populations — suggests around seven hours per night. On the surface, that sounds fine.

But we do not have RAND numbers for India. Not yet. And the direction of travel matters. India is urbanising at speed. Commutes in megacities are lengthening. The conditions that produced Japan's sleep crisis — long hours, financial stress, screen culture — are actively developing in India right now.

[VISUAL NOTE: India map. Fitbit data label. Cautionary note: "⚠️ ESTIMATED — No RAND-equivalent study for India. Wearable data has significant urban bias." Then: animated arrow showing India's urbanisation rate. Text: "The same conditions. A different timeline."]

The economic cost of sleep deprivation in India is not a current data point. It is a trajectory. And trajectories, unlike snapshots, are where the real cost is counted.

## CLOSE — (7:45–8:45)

Here is what makes the sleep deprivation crisis different from almost every other economic problem.

Most major economic challenges — inequality, inflation, housing — require policy, investment, political will, years of effort. They involve trade-offs.

Sleep deprivation has a fix. And the fix is free.

RAND modelled what happens if everyone sleeping under six hours per night shifts to just six to seven hours. Not a wellness retreat. Not a new drug. Just one more hour of sleep.

Four hundred and eleven billion dollars is what the United States is currently losing every year. RAND then modelled one specific intervention: just pushing everyone sleeping under six hours up to six to seven hours. That alone recovers two hundred and twenty-six billion dollars. The rest of the gap — getting those same people to seven hours or more — would recover the full four hundred and eleven billion. Two different targets. Both within reach.

[VISUAL NOTE: "Before and After" scenario bars. Current loss: $411B. Recovery scenario 1: $226B (push <6h sleepers to 6–7h). Recovery scenario 2: $411B (push all under-sleepers to 7h+). Clean animation. Label each bar clearly. Source: RAND RB-9962.]

Now here is the calculation you can run yourself.

Take your country's GDP. Find the percentage of the workforce sleeping under six hours per night — your national statistics office likely publishes this. Apply a loss coefficient between 1.3 and 2.3 percent. That is a rough but defensible estimate of what your economy is losing every year to tired workers.

The data is public. The methodology is documented. The problem is hiding in plain sight.

The world is losing hundreds of billions of dollars every year to something as fixable as going to bed an hour earlier. That is not a health crisis or an economics crisis. It is both. You are probably losing thousands of dollars a year in economic output — and you don't even know it. The cure costs nothing. It costs exactly one earlier bedtime.
