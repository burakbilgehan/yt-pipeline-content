## Key Findings

1. **NBA average salary grew ~288% (2000–2026):** from $3.4M (2000-01) to $13.2M (2025-26). The salary cap rose from $35.5M to $154.6M over the same period. [NotebookLM / Basketball-Reference / Spotrac]
2. **NBA max contracts tripled:** Kevin Garnett earned $19.6M as the highest-paid player in 2000-01; Steph Curry earns $59.6M in 2025-26 — a ~204% increase. [NotebookLM / Spotrac]
3. **Hollywood per-film pay hit 9 figures:** Tom Cruise earned $100M+ from three separate films (Top Gun: Maverick, Mission: Impossible — Dead Reckoning). Keanu Reeves made $156M from Matrix sequels. These numbers were unthinkable in 2000. [NotebookLM / Variety / Forbes]
4. **Streaming inflated actor pay further:** The Rock received $50M for Red One, Adam Sandler earns ~$48M/yr from Netflix, Jennifer Aniston earns $2M/episode on The Morning Show. Streamers overpay because they compete for subscribers, not box-office returns. [NotebookLM / Variety]
5. **Consumer entertainment costs outpaced inflation:** BLS "Admission to movies, theaters, concerts" index rose 105% between 2000 and 2025 — roughly double the ~86% general CPI increase over the same period. [BLS via NotebookLM]
6. **Netflix Standard price rose +150% in 15 years:** from $7.99/mo (2011) to $19.99/mo (2026). The Premium tier reached $26.99/mo, with yet another price hike in April 2026. Every major streamer followed suit. [Wikipedia / Netflix IR]
7. **Cable cord-cutting accelerated:** 76% of US adults had cable/satellite in 2015 → 36% in 2025 (Pew). Subscribers peaked at 68.5M (2000) and fell to ~66.1M broadly defined (2024), but the paying base shifted to streaming bundles costing $60-100+/mo combined. [Wikipedia / Pew Research]
8. **Real median household income barely moved:** $67,650 (1999) → $83,730 (2024) in real terms — roughly 24% over 25 years. Meanwhile, GDP per capita rose 67% since 1980 but median household income only 15%. The average consumer's purchasing power did not keep pace with entertainment cost inflation. [Wikipedia / FRED / US Census]
9. **Peak TV then shrinkflation:** Scripted original series surged from 182 (2002) to 559 (2021), then declined 24% by 2023. Seasons shortened from 22-24 episodes (network TV) to 8-12 (prestige/streaming). Consumers pay more for less content per show. No streaming series has ever reached 100 episodes. [Wikipedia / FX Networks]
10. **Net effect — wealth transfer:** Star earnings grew 200-300%, entertainment costs rose 100-240%, and real household income grew ~24%. The gap between what fans pay and what stars earn widened dramatically. This is a de facto capital transfer from the many to the few.

### Historical Context: How Pay Escalated

**2000 era:**
- Top-tier actors earned $20M/film (the "$20 million club": Tom Cruise, Jim Carrey, Will Smith, Julia Roberts)
- Backend deals existed but were rarer and less lucrative
- Studios controlled distribution and kept larger revenue shares

**2010s transition:**
- Franchise IP (Marvel, DC, Star Wars) shifted power from individual stars to brands
- But top actors negotiated backend deals on blockbusters → $50-100M paydays
- The rise of international box office (especially China) expanded the revenue pie

**2020s streaming era:**
- Streamers (Netflix, Amazon, Apple) compete for talent to drive subscriber growth
- No box office → no backend, so upfront pay inflated to compensate
- Netflix alone spent: $8B (2018) → $13.6B (2021) → $18.9B projected (2025) on content
- Result: more money to fewer stars for fewer episodes of shorter seasons

### Key Metric: Pay-Per-Episode Inflation

- Network TV (2000s): $75K-150K/episode for leads, 22-24 episodes/season
- Premium cable (2010s): $300K-1M/episode, 10-13 episodes/season
- Streaming prestige (2020s): $1-2M/episode, 8-10 episodes/season
- **Per-episode pay increased ~10-15x while episode counts dropped ~60%**

- **Pew headline stat:** 76% (2015) → 36% (2025) = **40 percentage points lost in 10 years**
- The "66.1M" figure for 2024 is misleading — it includes virtual MVPDs (YouTube TV, Hulu Live, etc.), which are effectively streaming services priced at $73-83/mo
- Traditional cable subscriber base has declined continuously since 2000

#### The ESPN Problem

- ESPN charges cable providers ~$7-10/month per subscriber — the highest carriage fee of any non-premium channel
- This means every cable subscriber (including those who never watch sports) subsidizes ESPN
- As subscribers leave cable, the per-subscriber cost pressure increases → prices rise for remaining subscribers → more leave (death spiral)
- ESPN's new standalone streaming service (launching 2025) at $24.99/mo represents the unbundling of this hidden subsidy

### Episode Shrinkflation

| Format | Typical Eps/Season | Typical Season Length |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Network TV (2000s) | 22-24 episodes | ~16-18 hours |

### Key Datapoints

- **Syndication threshold:** Traditional TV required 100 episodes for syndication deals. Streaming benchmark is 50 episodes total — but **no streaming-exclusive series has ever reached 100 episodes**.
- **Content per dollar:** A Netflix Standard subscription ($19.99/mo) in 2026 gets you access to shorter seasons of fewer shows than a $7.99 subscription in 2011 got — even though Netflix content spending nearly tripled ($8B → $18.9B).
- **Where the money goes:** Higher per-episode budgets, higher star pay, more expensive production values — but fewer total episodes. The consumer gets a more "premium" but shorter product at a higher price.
- **The irony:** During Peak TV (2014-2021), there was more content than anyone could watch. Now content is contracting while prices keep rising — a classic post-bubble squeeze.
